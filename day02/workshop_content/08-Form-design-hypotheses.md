# 08 · Form design hypotheses

**Goal:** commit to a prediction before you draw anything, and before you see any score.

This is what separates design from guessing. A hypothesis you wrote down can be wrong, and finding out that it was wrong is the useful part.

For each change you are considering, follow one chain of reasoning:

**observation → modification → predicted consequence → how it could fail**

| Hypothesis | Finish the sentence |
| --- | --- |
| Affinity | "Changing ___ could improve 5-HT2A binding because ___." |
| Selectivity | "The same change should be worse in 5-HT2B because ___." |
| Failure mode | "This could fail if ___, or if the ligand loses ___." |
| Expected pose | "A supportive pose would keep ___ and put ___ near ___." |

> **Discuss** — before you draw: decide as a group what a *supportive* docking result would look like. Not "a good score" — an actual pose. Which contact must survive? Where should your new group end up? Agreeing on this now is what lets you interpret the result later.
> {: .discuss }

Things that catch people out:

- Extra volume in a pocket is not automatically useful space.
- Adding a hydrogen-bond donor or acceptor costs desolvation. It does not always pay for itself.
- A group that makes a new contact may also break an existing one.
- Flexible substituents pay an entropic price that docking scores handle poorly.

Keep the designs simple. One or two changes from LSD means that when a score moves, you can say *why*. A molecule with six changes tells you nothing.

> **These are design ideas, not drug candidates.** Nothing here says anything about whether a molecule could be made, or what it would do in a cell or an animal.
> {: .warn }

**Checkpoint**

- [ ] You have written one affinity hypothesis and one selectivity hypothesis.
- [ ] Everyone in the group can explain why their change might work.
- [ ] Everyone can also name a plausible reason it will not.
- [ ] You have agreed what a supportive pose would look like.
